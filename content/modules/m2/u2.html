Why this matters

Scenario opener

A message from "IT Support" lands in your inbox with a polished logo and no spelling errors. It looks convincing until you expand the sender address and see a domain you do not recognize. The clue was in the structure, not the writing quality.

Core concept

Most phishing emails contain visible signals if you know where to look, but those signals live in the message structure rather than in obvious mistakes.

That matters because modern phishing often looks professional. Clean grammar and good branding do not prove legitimacy. Structural details such as sender, reply-to, and destination links are usually more reliable than tone or spelling.

What to inspect before you click

  1. 1
    Check the real sender. Display names are cosmetic; the actual address and domain matter more.
  2. 2
    Check reply behavior. A strange reply-to can redirect your response to the attacker.
  3. 3
    Check the destination. Hover or long-press links to see where the click would really go.
  4. 4
    Check the attachment context. Unexpected files deserve more suspicion than expected ones.
Workplace example

Why structure beats spelling

An attacker can copy your company's logo and write perfect English in seconds. They have a harder time making a fake domain, reply-to address, and login destination all fit the real workflow. That is why structural inspection is the higher-value habit.

What to do

Email triage rule

  • Do not trust a message only because it is clean and familiar-looking.
  • Expand senders and preview links before taking action.
  • Treat unexpected attachments as suspicious until the context checks out.

Exercise

Takeaway

Phishing emails reveal themselves through structure more reliably than through sloppy writing. Inspect the mechanics of the message, not just the surface.
