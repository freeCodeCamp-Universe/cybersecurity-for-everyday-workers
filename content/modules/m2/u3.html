Why this matters

Scenario opener

You ignore a suspicious email, then get a text from "the courier," a phone call from "IT," and a late-night MFA prompt you did not start. The channel changed, but the attack did not.

Core concept

Email is only one delivery path. Attackers also use text messages, phone calls, QR codes, and authentication prompts because each channel can feel more immediate and less filtered.

The defense does not need to change every time the channel changes. The core questions stay the same: who is asking, what are they asking for, and how can I verify this without using the suspicious message itself?

How the channels differ

Smishing

Texts feel personal and urgent, and mobile previews hide more detail than email clients do.

Vishing

Caller ID is easy to spoof, and voice pressure can feel more socially difficult to resist.

Quishing

QR codes hide the destination entirely until scanned, which makes them equivalent to blind links.

MFA fatigue

Repeated prompts are designed to wear you down into approving one by mistake.

Common mistake

People trust channels differently

Many users are more skeptical of email than of texts, calls, or QR codes. Attackers know that. A call can feel more real than an email. A QR code can feel more official than a link. The social pressure changes shape, but the verification rule should not.

What to do

One rule across every channel

  • Never approve an MFA prompt you did not start yourself.
  • Never give a code or password to a caller who contacted you first.
  • Treat unsolicited QR codes and shortened SMS links like unknown links.

Exercise

Takeaway

Phishing reaches you through any channel where decisions get made. The safer response is not memorizing channel-specific trivia; it is applying the same verification habit everywhere.
