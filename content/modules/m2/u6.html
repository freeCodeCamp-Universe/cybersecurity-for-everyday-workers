Why this matters

Scenario opener

Most workers cannot memorize every phishing trick, but they can apply two or three simple rules every time a message asks for something risky. That is what scales.

Core concept

A small, consistent verification habit catches more attacks than trying to remember every red flag.

This is the lesson that turns recognition into behavior. A habit is useful because it still works when you are tired, interrupted, on mobile, or dealing with a request that looks almost legitimate.

The core habit

  • Pause when a message tries to create speed.
  • Use a second channel for money, credentials, access, or sensitive data.
  • Navigate to important sites yourself instead of trusting links inside messages.
  • Report suspicious contact so the rest of the team benefits too.
Why habits work

Simple rules are more reliable than memory under pressure

You may not remember every clue about sender domains, QR-code behavior, or invoice fraud. But you can remember: "I never approve an MFA prompt I did not start" or "I never accept a banking change from email alone." Good rules remove decisions from the moment where attackers want confusion.

What to do

Write your own rules in plain language

Your rules should be short enough to repeat and concrete enough to act on. If the rule sounds vague, it will fail under pressure. If it names a trigger and a response, it is more likely to hold.

Exercise

Takeaway

Two or three verification rules, applied consistently, defeat many phishing attempts that make it past filters and into your workday.
