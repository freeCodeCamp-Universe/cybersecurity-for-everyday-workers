Why this matters

Scenario opener

You get an email from a real vendor contact with updated bank details and a short note asking you to handle it quietly before close of business. There is no suspicious link. That is exactly why targeted attacks work.

Core concept

Targeted attacks use real information about you, your role, or your company, so they bypass many of the signals that catch bulk phishing.

Instead of relying on sloppy wording or random mass mail, the attacker studies a workflow that already exists: payments, vendor changes, leadership requests, or account recovery. The defense therefore has to be procedural, not just perceptual.

The targeted-attack patterns to know

Spear phishing

Tailored to one person using public or stolen context so the message feels plausible.

BEC

Uses a trusted identity to trigger payment, secrecy, or account changes without obvious malware.

Vendor fraud

Abuses a real vendor relationship, often by compromising the vendor's account or imitating its domain.

Deepfake-assisted fraud

Adds voice or video realism to pressure a fast decision, but still collapses under proper verification.

What good looks like

The out-of-band rule

If a request changes money, credentials, banking details, or privileged access, verify it through a second channel you already trust. That means a saved number, a known chat thread, or an internal process you already use. It never means replying to the suspicious message and asking if it is real.

Common mistake

Trusting realism instead of process

A realistic message is not a verified message. Accurate titles, names, signatures, and even a convincing video call do not remove the need for a second-channel check. Good procedure is what defeats targeted attacks.

Exercise

Takeaway

When a request involves money, access, or credentials, realism is not enough. Procedure beats persuasion.
