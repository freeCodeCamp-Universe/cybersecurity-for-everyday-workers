Why this matters

Scenario opener

Owen is told to pick a "strong" password for a new HR system. He chooses MyDog2019! because it has a capital letter, numbers, and a symbol. It looks complicated, but it follows a pattern that attack tools already know extremely well.

Core concept

Password strength is mainly about length and unpredictability, not about adding cosmetic complexity to a short, familiar phrase.

That is why current guidance has shifted away from rigid complexity rules and toward longer passwords or passphrases. A short password with symbols can still be predictable. A long random passphrase gives attackers far more combinations to work through.

What actually makes a password strong

  1. 1
    Use length first. Every additional character increases the number of possible combinations an attacker must try.
  2. 2
    Use randomness, not personal meaning. Four random words are stronger than a quote, lyric, pet name, or date you would naturally remember.
  3. 3
    Keep rule-following minimal. If a site demands a capital letter or symbol, add it without shrinking the length or removing the randomness.
Common mistake

Why complexity rules mislead people

Rules like "one capital, one number, one symbol" were meant to make short passwords a little harder to guess. In practice, they trained people into a few repeated patterns: a capital first letter, a year at the end, and one symbol like !. Attack tools now generate those patterns automatically.

NIST SP 800-63B (2017, revised in 2022 draft) guidance favors allowing longer passwords and screening them against known breached-password lists. Some workplaces still require older-style complexity rules because of policy or compliance. When that happens, the best move is to keep the passphrase long and random, then satisfy the rule with one small change.

What good looks like

How to build a workable passphrase

  • Choose 4 or 5 random words, not a sentence you would normally say.
  • If required, add one capital letter and one symbol without shortening the phrase.
  • Avoid names, birthdays, seasons, company names, and keyboard patterns.
  • If the site rejects spaces, join the words without making them more predictable.

Exercise

Takeaway

A long, randomly built passphrase is stronger than a short, complicated-looking password. Build around length first, then satisfy any extra site rules with the smallest possible tweak.
