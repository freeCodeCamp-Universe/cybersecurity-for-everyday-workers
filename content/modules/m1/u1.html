Why this matters

Scenario opener

Maria uses the same strong password for her work email, payroll account, and favorite shopping site because it is long and hard to guess. A store she used two years ago is breached. She never hears about it, but attackers add her email and password to a list and start trying it everywhere else.

Core concept

Passwords fail not because people are careless, but because attackers use automated tools that exploit structural weaknesses at scale.

That matters because many people still judge password safety the way they judge a lock on a door: "Could a person guess this?" Attackers usually are not guessing by hand. They use breached lists, software, and scripts that test thousands or millions of possibilities very quickly.

How the common attacks work

Credential stuffing

Attackers take known email-and-password pairs from one breach and try them on other services. Reuse is what makes this work.

Dictionary attacks

Attack tools try common words, names, seasons, years, and predictable substitutions like p@ssw0rd or Welcome2025!.

Phishing

The attacker does not crack the password at all. They trick the user into typing it into a fake login page or giving it away directly.

Post-breach reuse

A password can remain dangerous for years after a breach because leaked credentials circulate long after the original incident.

Common mistake

The "I have nothing worth stealing" myth

Attackers do not need you to be rich or famous. A normal email account can reset other accounts. A work account can open the door to payroll, invoices, customer data, or internal systems. Even a low-value account can be used to send spam or phishing to other people.

The real question is not "Would someone target me personally?" It is "Would this account help someone make money, get access, or impersonate me?" The answer is often yes.

Workplace example

Why strong can still be weak

A single strong password reused across five services is safer than five weak passwords, but it still creates one shared point of failure. If any one of those services leaks it, the attacker does not need to guess anything else. They replay the same credential pair elsewhere.

This is why later units in this module focus on password managers, MFA, and passkeys. The fix is not just "try harder." It is to remove the pattern attackers depend on.

What to do

Immediate actions after this lesson

  • Identify any password you know you reuse across work or personal accounts.
  • Start with your email account, because it often controls password resets for everything else.
  • Assume older breaches still matter unless you have already changed the password everywhere it was reused.

Exercise

Takeaway

Passwords fail in predictable ways. Once you understand the method of failure, the later fixes in this module stop feeling like extra chores and start looking like direct defenses.
